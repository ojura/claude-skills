# Teaching and visual review

## Choose what the animation explains

Start from something students already recognize. Let motion answer a specific
question: where a basis vector lands, which component contributes to a
coordinate, what changes when frames are exchanged, or how scalar coefficients
become a matrix.

Before each reveal, leave enough information for a prediction. After the reveal,
hold a complete statement long enough to compare it with the geometry. Give
each new representation a reason to exist in the derivation.

When reorganizing scalar equations into a matrix, keep the coefficient identity
visible throughout the move. Explicitly identify the input and output
coordinates. Show columns as transformed basis vectors when that interpretation
fits the material. Distinguish a change of coordinates from an active rotation
through the notation and the actual diagram.

When manual inversion is used to motivate a linear operator, emphasize the
burden of the expanded calculation and the simpler operation that follows.
Check the inverse relation independently before animating it. Allocate the
intermediate detail according to the lecturer's intended discussion.

## Use motion and emphasis deliberately

Move quantities whose identity persists. Introduce a newly defined quantity
with a distinct appearance or an explicit replacement. Keep reference geometry
visible at reduced emphasis when students still need it to interpret the
current step.

Use a consistent colour for a mathematical role across geometry, labels and
equations. Draw from the repository palette so both themes remain readable.
Check faint guides and older bullets on an actual projector-sized view.

Avoid simultaneous movements that require students to track unrelated changes.
If a symbol's position changes for layout, make that distinct from a geometric
transformation. Check text at the start, middle and end of each motion for
collisions, clipping, lingering glyphs and unexpected fills.

## Keep geometric annotations complete

Build related geometric marks with one shared constructor and migrate comparable
call sites to it. A marked angle must own an explicit zero-direction reference
ray, its arc and a semantic label. Derive the ray and arc from the same origin
and start direction. Transform the complete group so the ray starts at the arc
center and points along the arc's start direction after translation, rotation
or scaling.

Inspect exported intermediate and transformed states in both themes. Include a
state where the annotation separates from any coincident axis, and verify each
required component's position and visible stroke or text. Keep a regression
that fails when a required component is removed or hidden while checkpoint
metadata remains valid.

## Review the printed slide independently

A printable derivation needs an explicit reading order and a complete final
result. Number panels and place captions consistently. Reuse text and geometry
from the scene, while allowing a compact arrangement for the available space.

Inspect the handout at ordinary reading size. Check that labels, mathematical
symbols, figure references and the final equation remain legible. A panel count
that worked in another scene may be unsuitable for this one.

Replacement photographs and figures need one chosen handout state. Ordinary
overlay collapse can combine several full-size images on the same page; inspect
the actual compiled handout wherever the source uses replacement overlays.

## Review with the lecturer's controls

A checkpoint must support a pause for explanation. The previous-step action
should recover a useful state after an accidental advance. Replay should restore
the intended initial state. Continuous play should make sense as a complete
demonstration while normal Next preserves the teaching stops.

Read the visible presenter controls and configured bindings from the actual
viewer. Verify page labels and logical slide navigation after adding overlays.
Check that the audience view and presenter's current slide show the same state,
and that the next-slide preview remains useful.

For pacing, watch the complete sequence at normal speed after inspecting
individual frames. Consider what the lecturer needs to say at each pause and
what a student must visually follow during each movement.
